Case Study · CAPA & Laboratory Investigations

The Standard That Failed the Samples

An SDS-PAGE degradation OOS caused by a weak reference standard, and how each root cause analysis tool was chosen along the way.

Illustrative teaching case. This is a composite case inspired by real laboratory investigation experience. Product and numerical details have been changed, and the investigation is presented as a model of good practice. It does not describe any specific company, product, or event. All data are illustrative.

At a glance

Product / testSDS-PAGE identity and degradation test for a therapeutic protein, quantified by densitometry
SpecificationDegradation < 5%
The problemTwo lots read 6–9% degradation, although the gel looked normal by eye
Root causeA deteriorated degraded standard ran lighter than normal. Because the method sets scan sensitivity for the whole gel against that standard, every sample lane was magnified.
Why it wasn’t caughtThe standard’s preparation date wasn’t recorded, so its 30-day expiry couldn’t be verified or enforced
How it was provenRetest of the same samples under a pre-approved protocol, with the old and a new standard side by side
OutcomeAssignable cause documented; OOS invalidated with QA approval; lots released; SOP revised; CAPA effective

How the test works

Each gel has ten lanes: a molecular-weight (MW) standard, a positive control, a negative control, a blank, and two sample lots in triplicate. A degraded standard, a protein sample with a known amount of breakdown, serves as the system suitability check.

After staining, the gel is scanned on a calibrated densitometer. Per the validated SOP, the analyst raises the scan sensitivity for the whole gel until the degraded standard shows at least 5% degradation, but stops before the negative control shows any. Software then calculates degradation in each sample lane. The key point: the sensitivity setting applies to every lane at once.

1. The signal

On a routine release gel, both sample lots read between 6.1% and 8.8% degradation across their triplicate lanes: out of specification. Yet when the analyst and reviewer looked at the gel itself, the sample lanes looked like every passing gel before them: a strong main band and only faint minor bands.

Tool decision · First look

Situation
OOS results from the software, but the gel looks normal.
Tool chosen
Visual review of the gel alongside the calculated results.
Why
A quick check of whether the picture and the number agree before doing anything else.
Pros
Fast and free. It exposed the key contradiction: normal-looking lanes, high numbers.
Cons / limits
Subjective. It can’t overrule a validated quantitative result, only raise questions.
Also considered
An immediate retest. Rejected: without a hypothesis, a retest risks “testing into compliance.”

2. Contain and define

  • Contain. Both lots placed on hold; QA notified the same day under the OOS procedure.
  • Preserve evidence. The gel image, the scan file with its settings, retained sample portions, and the degraded standard vial in use were kept and labeled.
  • Define. The team wrote a problem statement and an Is / Is-Not.

Tool decision · Define the problem

Situation
Contradictory evidence: normal gel, failing numbers.
Tool chosen
Kepner-Tregoe Is / Is-Not.
Why
Maps where the problem is and isn’t, so the pattern points to the cause.
Pros
Showed the failure affected every sample lane, and that only one control lane was abnormal.
Cons / limits
Organizes facts; doesn’t prove anything by itself.
Also considered
A fishbone diagram. Useful as a checklist, but too broad once the pattern pointed to the standard.

3. Was the run valid?

The first question in any OOS is whether the test itself worked. The MW standard separated normally, the positive control showed its expected band, and the negative control and blank were clean. The degraded standard met the SOP’s suitability window: it reached 5% before the negative control showed any degradation.

So the run was technically valid under the SOP, and the OOS results couldn’t simply be set aside. The investigation had to find a cause.

Tool decision · Run validity

Situation
Controls look normal, but the degraded standard is light.
Tool chosen
System suitability review, lane by lane.
Why
An invalid run is handled differently from an OOS, so this must be settled first.
Pros
Uses data already on the gel; clarified that the results stood until explained.
Cons / limits
A “window” criterion can be met even when the standard is weak, so passing suitability didn’t mean everything was normal.
Also considered
Declaring the run invalid. Rejected: the written criteria were met.

4. Finding the mechanism

The software record showed the scan sensitivity used for this gel was 78, compared with a typical range of 40–55 on recent passing gels. Because the degraded standard was light, the analyst had to raise sensitivity much further than usual to make it show 5%.

That explained everything at once. Higher sensitivity magnifies faint signal in every lane. The negative control, with nothing to detect, stayed at 0%. But the samples’ small, normal minor bands and background were magnified into 6–9% “degradation.”

Working hypothesis

A weak degraded standard forced an unusually high gel-wide sensitivity setting, which inflated the degradation results for normal samples.

Tool decision · Explain the pattern

Situation
The standard is light, and sensitivity is set against it for the whole gel.
Tool chosen
Mechanism analysis: following the method step by step, backed by the software’s record of the sensitivity setting.
Why
A single mechanism that explains every observation is a strong candidate cause.
Pros
Tied all the facts together; the audit trail gave objective evidence of an unusual setting.
Cons / limits
A plausible explanation still needs an experiment to confirm it.
Also considered
Re-analyzing the original scan at a lower sensitivity. Rejected: changing processing settings until results pass looks like testing into compliance.

5. Phase I laboratory investigation

Reagents and gel-wide factors

Stain, destain, buffers, and the gel itself were early suspects. But these act on the whole gel, and every other lane looked normal. Only the degraded standard lane was light, which pointed to a lane-specific cause: the standard itself, its preparation, or its loading.

Tool decision · Reagents or the standard?

Situation
One lane abnormal; all others normal.
Tool chosen
Is / Is-Not reasoning on the lane pattern.
Why
Gel-wide causes can’t explain a single weak lane.
Pros
Instant and free; eliminated a whole family of causes (stain, destain, buffer, gel, run conditions).
Cons / limits
Narrows the search but doesn’t prove the cause.
Also considered
Testing each reagent lot. Unnecessary once the pattern ruled out gel-wide causes.

Records and SOP review

  • Analyst interview: preparation, loading, and staining followed the SOP; nothing unusual noticed except the faint standard.
  • Densitometer: within calibration.
  • SOP: the degraded standard is stored frozen between uses and is good for 30 days.
  • Gap found: the standard’s preparation date was not recorded anywhere, so its age, and whether it was within its 30-day expiry, couldn’t be determined. The vial had been thawed and refrozen for each use, with no limit on freeze-thaw cycles.

Tool decision · Why was the standard weak?

Situation
The standard is the prime suspect; age and handling are likely factors.
Tool chosen
Records and SOP review.
Why
Storage and expiry rules are the fastest thing to check.
Pros
Exposed a system gap: an expiry that couldn’t be verified or enforced.
Cons / limits
Missing records meant age couldn’t be confirmed from paperwork. The question had to be answered by experiment.
Also considered
Change Analysis across standard lots. Limited here, because the standard’s history wasn’t recorded.

6. Proving the cause

QA approved a written protocol before any retesting. It stated the hypothesis (a weak degraded standard inflated the results), specified a retest of the same retained samples, and put the old standard and a freshly prepared standard on the same gel. Sensitivity was set per the SOP using the new standard.

LaneResultWhat it shows
New degraded standardNormal intensity; reached 5% at sensitivity 46Sensitivity back in the typical range
Old degraded standardVisibly lighter; read only 2.7% at the same settingThe old standard had lost signal
Lot 1 (triplicate)1.9%, 2.1%, 2.3%Passes; consistent with history
Lot 2 (triplicate)2.0%, 2.2%, 2.4%Passes; consistent with history
MW standard, controls, blankNormalValid run

With a normal standard, sensitivity returned to its usual range and both lots passed. Running the old standard on the same gel showed directly that it had weakened. That side-by-side comparison turned a likely explanation into a demonstrated cause.

Supporting check (why it weakened): fresh standard aliquots put through repeated freeze-thaw cycles lost visible intensity after several cycles, consistent with the old vial’s thaw-and-refreeze history.

Tool decision · Confirm the cause

Situation
A strong hypothesis, but no records to confirm the standard’s age.
Tool chosen
Hypothesis-driven retest under a pre-approved protocol, with a paired comparison (old vs. new standard on the same gel).
Why
Changes only the suspect and compares it directly with a known-good version.
Pros
One run answered the key questions; FDA-compliant, not testing into compliance.
Cons / limits
Retesting the same samples assumes they were stored properly (they were retained under controlled conditions).
Also considered
Resampling the lots. Not needed: the evidence pointed to the test, not the samples.

7. Method or input?

The method lets the standard set the scale for the whole gel, so a weak standard can inflate every result. The team considered redesigning the method but decided against it. SDS-PAGE with densitometry is semi-quantitative by nature, the bracketing approach is common practice, and the method was validated. Revalidation would be costly and wouldn’t remove the reliance on a good standard.

Instead, the team chose to control the input: make sure the degraded standard is always within its expiry and in good condition, and make unusual sensitivity settings visible.

Tool decision · Fix the method or the input?

Situation
The method is sensitive to the standard’s condition.
Tool chosen
Risk-based decision (ICH Q9 principles).
Why
Match the fix to the risk and the cost.
Pros
Fast, proportionate, no revalidation.
Cons / limits
The method’s dependence on the standard remains; controls must stay effective.
Also considered
Method redesign and revalidation. Rejected as disproportionate.

8. Root cause statement

TriggerThe degraded standard had deteriorated (lost signal) after repeated freeze-thaw cycles over an untracked period of use.
MechanismBecause sensitivity is set for the whole gel against the standard, the analyst raised it well above normal, magnifying faint signal in every sample lane.
Why it wasn’t prevented or detectedThe standard’s preparation date wasn’t recorded, so the 30-day expiry couldn’t be enforced. There was no freeze-thaw limit, and no check on how high the sensitivity setting had to go.

Tool decision · Get to the system cause

Situation
The technical cause is confirmed.
Tool chosen
5 Whys.
Why
To find why the weak standard wasn’t prevented or noticed.
Pros
Led from “weak standard” to “expiry not traceable” and “no sensitivity check,” which the CAPA can fix.
Cons / limits
Only reliable after the technical cause is proven; used earlier, it can build on a guess.
Also considered
Fault tree analysis. More structure than this problem needed.

9. Disposition of the OOS results

  • QA approved invalidation of the original OOS results, with the protocol, gel images, and sensitivity records attached as the assignable cause.
  • Both lots were released on the passing retest results.
  • Look-back: other gels run with the old standard were reviewed. A weak standard pushes results higher, never lower, so earlier passing results couldn’t have hidden a failure. No other lots were affected.

10. CAPA

TypeAction
CorrectionOld degraded standard discarded; fresh standard prepared.
Corrective actionSOP revised: the degraded standard’s preparation date is recorded on the results form, so the reviewer can confirm it is within its 30-day expiry.
Corrective actionStandard vials labeled with preparation date, expiry, and preparer.
Preventive actionStandard prepared as single-use aliquots, so no vial is thawed and refrozen.
Preventive actionThe sensitivity setting is recorded for every gel, with a typical range. A setting outside that range triggers a check of the standard before results are approved.
TrainingAnalysts and reviewers trained on the revised SOP and on why a weak standard can inflate results.

11. Effectiveness check

Criteria were set before closing: over the next 30 days (one full standard life cycle) and at least 10 gels, no OOS results linked to the standard, the standard’s preparation date recorded on 100% of results forms, and every sensitivity setting within the typical range. All criteria were met, and the CAPA was closed as effective.

Key takeaways

  1. When the picture and the number disagree, investigate the number. Normal-looking lanes were the first sign the problem was in the measurement.
  2. Know how your method scales. When one reference sets the sensitivity for the whole gel, a weak reference distorts every result.
  3. Let the lane pattern guide you. Gel-wide causes can’t explain a single weak lane.
  4. An expiry you can’t verify isn’t a control. Recording the preparation date turned a rule into something reviewers can check.
  5. Put the suspect next to a known-good version. The side-by-side standards made the cause visible.
  6. Fix what matters in proportion. Controlling the standard solved the problem without revalidating the method.
  7. Know which way the bias runs. A weak standard only inflates results, which kept the look-back simple.

Questions to think about

  1. The run passed system suitability. Why couldn’t the OOS results be invalidated on that basis alone?
  2. Why is re-analyzing the original scan at a lower sensitivity not an acceptable fix?
  3. What made running the old and new standards on the same gel so much stronger than using only the new one?
  4. Which reagents or standards in your lab have expiries that aren’t actually tracked?
  5. When would it be worth redesigning or revalidating the method instead of controlling the standard?
